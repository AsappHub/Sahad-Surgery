Types of Hemorrhage

What is hemorrhage?

Hemorrhage is the escape of blood from the blood vessels.

It is classified in four ways: by the source of bleeding, by type, by duration and by onset of bleeding.

Based on source of bleeding

Which type of blood vessel is bleeding.

Arterial hemorrhage
  • Blood comes from an artery.
  • Bright red in colour.
  • Spurts out in time with the pulse.
  • High pressure, so it is hard to control and blood is lost quickly.
Venous hemorrhage
  • Blood comes from a vein.
  • Dark red in colour.
  • Flows out steadily and continuously.
  • Lower pressure, so it is usually easier to control with firm pressure.
Capillary hemorrhage
  • Blood comes from the tiny capillaries.
  • Slow ooze from the whole raw surface.
  • Often stops on its own or with light pressure.

Based on types of hemorrhage

Whether the blood can be seen.

Revealed hemorrhage
  • Blood is visible outside the body.
  • Examples: bleeding from a wound, nosebleed, coughing up blood, vomiting blood.
Concealed hemorrhage
  • Blood collects inside the body and cannot be seen.
  • Examples: bleeding into the abdomen, chest or deep tissues.
  • Signs of shock may be the only clue, so it can be missed.
Initially concealed but later revealed
  • Bleeding starts inside and shows outside later.
  • Example: a bleeding stomach ulcer fills the gut first, then appears as vomited blood or black tarry stool.

Based on duration of hemorrhage

How long the bleeding goes on.

Acute hemorrhage
  • Sudden loss of a large amount of blood in a short time.
  • Can quickly lead to shock.
Chronic hemorrhage
  • Small amounts of blood lost again and again over a long time.
  • May go unnoticed and lead to anaemia.
Acute on chronic hemorrhage
  • A sudden heavy bleed on top of long-standing slow bleeding.
  • The patient is already anaemic, so tolerates it poorly.

Based on onset of bleeding

When the bleeding starts in relation to the injury or operation.

Primary hemorrhage
  • Occurs at the time of the injury or operation.
Reactionary hemorrhage
  • Occurs within 24 hours, commonly in the first 4 to 6 hours.
  • Blood pressure rises as the patient recovers from shock.
  • A tied vessel may slip its ligature, or a clot may be dislodged.
Secondary hemorrhage
  • Occurs about 7 to 14 days later.
  • Usually due to infection that weakens and erodes the vessel wall.
  • Other causes: pressure from a drain, or spread of a tumour.
